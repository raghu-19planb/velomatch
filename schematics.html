Sheet 005Bike schematics

Know your bike before you buy one.

Bikes are getting more complex — electronic shifting, wireless derailleurs, a dozen cassette options that all sound the same in a shop pitch. Watch a bike go together, part by part, so you can walk in speaking the language instead of nodding along.

  • Frame

    Ties every other part together — its size and angles set how the bike fits and handles.

  • Fork & headset

    Holds the front wheel and lets it steer; the headset is the bearing that lets the fork rotate smoothly.

  • Wheels

    Rim, spokes, and hub — sized by diameter (700c, 650b, 26", etc.) to match the frame and tires.

  • Handlebar, stem & levers

    Where you steer and brake from. The stem sets reach and height; the same lever often brakes and shifts.

  • Saddle & seatpost

    The post sets your height; the saddle is the single most personal fit point on the whole bike.

  • Crankset & pedals

    Where your legs put in power — the chainring's tooth count sets how hard or easy each stroke feels.

  • Chain

    Carries power from the crankset to whichever rear cog is currently selected.

  • Brakes

    Slows the wheel — drawn here as rim calipers, though many modern bikes use disc brakes instead.

  • Rear cog

    One gear, always engaged — no derailleur, cable, or adjustment needed. Common on fixed-gear and single-speed bikes.

  • Cassette

    A stack of different-sized cogs. Shifting moves the chain sideways between them to change gear.

  • Rear derailleur

    A cable-pulled arm that pushes the chain onto a bigger or smaller cog when you shift.

  • Gear cable housing

    Carries the pull from your shift lever to the derailleur — the part that stretches over time and needs the odd adjustment.

  • Cassette — narrow range (11–28T)

    Gears sit close together for smooth, evenly-spaced pedaling on flatter roads — but no easy bail-out gear for steep climbs.

  • Cassette — wide range (10–50T)

    Trades smooth spacing for a much easier bail-out gear — the difference between grinding and walking on a loaded climb.

  • Rear derailleur — short cage

    A narrow cassette needs less chain wrap, so the derailleur's cage — the part holding the two small pulley wheels — stays short.

  • Rear derailleur — long cage

    A wide-range cassette needs far more chain wrap to take up slack, so the cage stretches noticeably longer to reach the biggest cog.

  • Gear cable housing

    Carries the pull from your shift lever to the derailleur, whichever range the cassette is built for.

  • Cassette

    Same job as on a mechanical bike — a stack of cogs the chain moves between — usually with more speeds (11–13) on electronic groupsets.

  • Electronic rear derailleur

    A small motor moves the chain instead of a cable — shifts stay precise and never need cable-tension adjustment.

  • Battery pack

    Powers the derailleur's motor (and front derailleur, if fitted) — typically good for hundreds of kilometres per charge.

  • Wireless shift signal

    A button tap sends a signal — wireless on some brands, a thin wire on others — to the derailleur's motor instead of pulling a cable.

Understanding your bike is how you avoid overpaying for the wrong thing.

A shop can always describe a bike in terms that sound impressive. Knowing what each part actually does is how you tell an upgrade worth paying for from one that isn't.

01

Same words, no surprises

"Cassette," "derailleur cage," "electronic shifting" stop being sales jargon and start being parts you can point at.

02

Spot the real trade-offs

Wider cassette, longer derailleur cage, heavier bike — every upgrade trades something for something else. Now you can see what.

03

Ask better questions

To everyone who's stood in a bike aisle guessing,

Buying a bike usually comes down to a guess: a size sticker on a rack, a salesperson pointing at whatever's in stock, or a color you liked in a catalog. Get it wrong and it's money spent on something that never quite fits.

We think fit should come from geometry, not guesswork — reach, stack, standover, all measured and matched to the rider, whoever that rider is.

VeloMatch is built to do that matching for every kind of rider and every kind of ride. Right now it's four working prototypes: a first bike for a growing kid, a daily commuter, a bikepacking rig, and a racing or ultra-endurance bike — because those are the cases where a wrong guess costs the most. The same approach — real measurements, scored against real bikes — is what we're building toward next, for every rider after this one.

It won't get everything right yet. But it will show its work — the frame size, the gear capacity, the climbing range — so you're choosing a bike, not gambling on one.
