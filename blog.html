SizingFor parents

How to actually measure a kid for their first bike

Brands publish a height range for each bike, and that is a good first filter: it is what the kids' quiz uses. But two children of the same height can have different leg lengths, and the leg is what decides whether a bike fits. Here is the quick way to check.

1. Measure their inseam. Have your child stand barefoot against a wall with their feet slightly apart. Press a book, spine up, snugly between their legs, the way a saddle would sit. Measure from the floor to the top of the book. That number is the inseam.

2. Compare it with the bike. Many sellers list a bike's lowest seat height and its standover height (how tall the frame is where your child stands over it). The seat's lowest setting should be at or below the inseam, so your child can reach the ground, and they should be able to stand over the frame with a little room to spare. If the seller does not list these numbers, ask: they are normal questions.

3. Check the reach. Small hands need to reach the brake levers comfortably, and the handlebars should not make your child stretch.

4. Do not buy too big. A bike a child has to "grow into" is harder to control and can put them off. Choose a bike that fits now and has some adjustment left in the seat and handlebars. The quiz shows how many centimetres of growing room each bike has at your child's current height, so you can see that trade-off.

5. Measure again. Children grow in spurts. Re-check the seat height and the signs in the "outgrown" note every few months.

This is general fitting guidance, not a replacement for the maker's own size chart.

Learning to rideFor parents

Balance bike or training wheels?

Both get a child riding. They teach different parts of it first.

A balance bike has no pedals. A child sits, pushes along with their feet and learns to balance and steer. Many parents and coaches like it because balance is usually the hardest part to learn, and pedals can come later. Some kids move from a balance bike straight to a pedal bike with no training wheels.

Training wheels keep the bike upright so a child can learn to pedal and brake. They do not teach balance, and a child may still need to learn that when the wheels come off.

How to choose. A child who is keen to run and glide often does well on a balance bike. A child who already has a pedal bike may be fine continuing with training wheels and lifting them as they gain confidence. Children vary, and either route works. What matters most is a bike that fits and a child who is enjoying it.

In the kids' quiz, tell us which your child has used: it changes which bikes we suggest.

Growing with themFor parents

Signs they have outgrown their bike

Children rarely say "this bike is too small". Look for these instead:

The seat is at its highest setting and their legs still look bent and cramped at the bottom of the pedal stroke, or they have to sit forward on the seat.

Their knees come up near the handlebars as they pedal.

They are stretched or hunched to reach the handlebars or brake levers, or they ride with their knees out to the sides.

They no longer have clearance when they stand over the frame, or they can barely reach the ground.

They have stopped wanting to ride. Sometimes the bike simply stopped being comfortable.

If several of these are true, check the brand's size chart for the next wheel size up. The quiz can then show you which bikes fit your child now.

To everyone who's stood in a bike aisle guessing,

Buying a bike usually comes down to a guess: a size sticker on a rack, a salesperson pointing at whatever's in stock, or a color you liked in a catalog. Get it wrong and it's money spent on something that never quite fits.

We think fit should come from geometry, not guesswork — reach, stack, standover, all measured and matched to the rider, whoever that rider is.

SelectVelo is built to do that matching for every kind of rider and every kind of ride. Right now it's five working prototypes: a first bike for a growing kid, a daily commuter, a bikepacking rig, a mountain bike, and a racing or ultra-endurance bike — because those are the cases where a wrong guess costs the most. The same approach — real measurements, scored against real bikes — is what we're building toward next, for every rider after this one.

It won't get everything right yet. But it will show its work — the wheel size, the standover, the years of growth room — so you're choosing a bike, not gambling on one.
